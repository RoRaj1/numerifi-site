Investing & the Markets

Explain stocks, bonds, and why diversification manages risk. ~25 min

What a stock and a bond actually are

A stock is a small slice of ownership in a company. Buy one share of a company and you own a tiny piece of it — if the company grows more valuable, your share usually does too; if it struggles, so does your share's value. There's no promised return and no guarantee you get your money back; you're a part-owner, for better or worse.

A bond is the opposite relationship: you're lending money, not owning anything. Buy a bond from a company or government and you're paid back over time with interest, on a schedule fixed when you bought it. Bonds are generally more predictable than stocks, but that predictability usually comes with a lower long-run return. The core trade-off running through almost all of investing is right here: ownership (stocks) tends to pay more over time but swings harder; lending (bonds) is steadier but usually pays less.

Risk, return, and diversification

"Risk" in investing mostly means how much a value can swing before it reaches its long-run average — not necessarily "chance of losing everything." Cash barely moves at all and barely grows. Bonds move a little more and tend to pay a little more. Stocks move the most and, over long periods, have historically paid the most. None of this is a guarantee for any specific year — it's a pattern over long stretches of time, with plenty of bad years mixed in.

Diversification means holding many different investments instead of one, so a bad outcome in any single company doesn't sink the whole portfolio. A fund that holds pieces of hundreds of companies will rarely go to zero, even though any one of those companies individually could. This is the entire reason index funds and mutual funds exist: they bundle many stocks or bonds into one purchase, spreading the risk automatically.

The dashed ring shows a single stock's wider range of outcomes — diversified holdings narrow that range without necessarily lowering the average.

How markets work

A stock exchange (like the NYSE or Nasdaq) is where buyers and sellers meet to trade shares; prices move constantly based on what people are willing to pay. An index (like the S&P 500) tracks a basket of stocks to represent "the market" as a whole, so you can gauge overall performance without watching every company. A bull market describes a sustained period of rising prices; a bear market describes a sustained decline. Both are normal parts of a market cycle — corrections and recoveries have happened repeatedly throughout market history.

A stylized, illustrative index path — not any specific index or a prediction.

Where AI fits into investing today

AI shows up in investing in a few concrete ways already: robo-advisors that build and rebalance a diversified portfolio automatically based on your goals and risk tolerance, tools that scan news and earnings reports for sentiment faster than a person could, and risk models that flag unusual patterns in a portfolio. None of this replaces the fundamentals in this module — risk, return, and diversification still decide the outcome. What changes is the speed and scale at which decisions get made. Workshops is where you'll actually use these tools on a mock portfolio, so consider this module the vocabulary you'll need to make sense of what you're seeing.
