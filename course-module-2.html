Saving & Compound Interest

Explain compound interest and pick a smart savings account. ~15 min

Pay yourself first

"Pay yourself first" means treating savings like a bill that comes due the moment money arrives, not whatever's left over after spending. If you wait to save until the end of the month, there's usually nothing left — not because you're bad with money, but because spending expands to fill whatever's available. Moving a fixed amount to savings first, even $10, flips that: now spending has to fit around savings, instead of the other way around.

Part of that savings should build an emergency fund — money set aside strictly for something unplanned: a phone repair, a missed shift, a medical copay. It's not for a sale on sneakers. Even a small emergency fund (a few hundred dollars) is often the difference between a bad week and a bad month, because it keeps a surprise expense from turning into high-interest debt.

How compound interest works

Interest is the bank paying you for letting it use your money. Compound interest means the interest you earn also earns interest, so a balance doesn't grow in a straight line — it curves upward, slowly at first, then faster. $100 at 6% annual interest becomes $106 after year one. In year two, interest is calculated on $106, not $100. The gap between simple and compound growth looks small at first and becomes large given enough time, which is why starting early matters more than starting with more money.

The Rule of 72: divide 72 by an interest rate to estimate how many years it takes money to double. At 6% interest, money roughly doubles every 72 ÷ 6 = 12 years. It's a mental-math shortcut, not an exact formula, but it's accurate enough to compare options quickly.

Same monthly amount, same return — the only difference is a 10-year head start. Illustrative example, not a guaranteed return.

Picking a savings account

Not all savings accounts pay the same interest. A standard savings account at a big bank might pay well under 1%; a high-yield savings account, usually from an online bank, can pay meaningfully more, with no extra risk — it's still insured, still your money, still withdrawable. When comparing accounts, look at the interest rate (often called APY, annual percentage yield), any minimum balance requirements, and whether there's a monthly fee that could eat the interest you're earning.
