Money Mindset & Budgeting

Build a working budget and tell needs from wants. ~20 min

Why financial literacy matters

Most people learn how money actually works by making expensive mistakes with their own money — a missed payment, an overdrawn account, a credit card balance that snowballs. Schools teach algebra and literature, but rarely how to read a paycheck or compare a 0% and a 22% APR. That gap isn't an accident; personal finance was added to very few state curricula until recently, so you're probably starting close to zero, and that's the normal starting point, not a deficiency.

The goal of this course isn't to make you an expert by the end of two hours. It's to get you comfortable with the vocabulary and the handful of ideas — budgeting, interest, credit, risk — that show up in almost every financial decision you'll make for the rest of your life. Everything else builds on this module.

Needs vs. wants, and the 50/30/20 rule

A need is something you'd keep paying for even if money got tight: rent, groceries, a phone bill, gas to get to work. A want is something that makes life better but that you could cut without real harm: streaming subscriptions, eating out, new clothes you don't need yet. Most money stress doesn't come from needs — it comes from wants quietly growing until they crowd out saving.

The 50/30/20 rule is a starting split, not a law: roughly 50% of your after-tax income toward needs, 30% toward wants, and 20% toward savings and debt payoff. It's a rough guide for people with money coming in regularly — your actual numbers might be 70/20/10 some months, and that's fine. What matters is that you're deciding the split on purpose instead of finding out at the end of the month where it went.

● Needs 50%  ● Wants 30%  ● Savings 20%

Building your first budget

A budget is just a plan for money that hasn't been spent yet, written down before you spend it instead of reconstructed afterward from memory. The simplest version has three steps: list what comes in each month, list what has to go out, and decide what's left over before it disappears into "wants" by default.

Worked example: say you earn $400/month from a part-time job. Needs (phone plan, gas, lunch money) run about $180. That leaves $220. Using the 50/30/20 idea loosely, you might put $120 toward wants (going out, games, clothes) and set aside $100 in savings. The number matters less than the habit: decide the split before the money arrives, not after it's gone.

Try it yourself: write down everything you spent money on last month. Sort each item into Needs or Wants. Most people are surprised by which column ends up bigger.
