Credit & Debt

Read a credit score and compare debt payoff strategies. ~20 min

What a credit score is, and why it follows you

A credit score is a number, roughly 300 to 850, that summarizes how reliably you've paid back money you've borrowed. Lenders use it to decide whether to approve a loan or credit card, and at what interest rate. A higher score usually means lower interest rates — on a car loan or eventually a mortgage, a 100-point difference in credit score can mean thousands of dollars in extra interest over the life of the loan. Landlords and even some employers check it too.

The score is built mostly from: payment history (do you pay on time?), amounts owed (how much of your available credit are you using?), length of credit history, and the mix and recency of new credit. None of it is permanent — a score moves as your habits do, in both directions.

Credit cards: APR, statements, and minimum payments

A credit card isn't free money — it's a short-term loan that resets every billing cycle. APR (annual percentage rate) is the yearly cost of borrowing if you carry a balance; many cards sit around 20–25%. If you pay your full statement balance by the due date, most cards charge no interest at all. The trouble starts with the minimum payment: it's designed to keep the account in good standing, not to pay off the balance. Paying only the minimum on a $1,000 balance at 22% APR can take years to clear and cost more in interest than the original purchase.

Reading a statement matters as much as having the card: check the due date, the statement balance, the minimum payment, and the APR every cycle. A card is a tool for building credit history and earning convenience or rewards — it's the balance you don't pay off that turns it into a problem.

Loans, payoff strategies, and predatory lending

When you do carry debt across more than one account, two common payoff strategies are the snowball method (pay minimums on everything, put extra money toward the smallest balance first, for quick psychological wins) and the avalanche method (put extra money toward the highest-interest balance first, which saves the most money overall). Avalanche is cheaper in total interest; snowball is easier for some people to stick with. Either beats paying minimums on everything indefinitely.

Predatory lending red flag: payday loans and "no credit check" loans often carry APRs in the triple digits — not a typo. If a loan is marketed as fast, easy, and doesn't ask about your ability to repay, treat that as a warning sign, not a convenience.
