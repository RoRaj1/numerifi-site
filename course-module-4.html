Banking, Paychecks & Taxes

Read a paycheck and explain how tax brackets work. ~15 min

Bank accounts & digital payments

A checking account is for money you'll use soon: paying bills, debit card purchases, daily spending. A savings account is for money you're setting aside, usually paying more interest in exchange for fewer transactions. Most banks let you hold both, and moving money between them is often instant and free. Digital wallets (Apple Pay, Google Pay) and payment apps (Venmo, Cash App) move money between people or pay at checkout without a physical card, but they aren't bank accounts — the balance sitting in some of these apps isn't always insured the way a bank account is, so moving spare balances to an actual bank account is usually safer.

Reading a paycheck

Gross pay is what you earned before anything is taken out. Net pay (what actually hits your account) is gross pay minus taxes and any other deductions. The gap between the two is usually federal income tax, Social Security and Medicare (together called FICA), and sometimes state income tax. A W-2 is the form your employer sends each January summarizing a full year of wages and taxes withheld — you'll need it to file a tax return.

A sample $1,000 gross paycheck. Exact splits vary by state and withholding elections.

How tax brackets actually work

The U.S. uses a progressive tax system, taxed in slices, not all-or-nothing. A common misconception: "if I earn enough to enter the 22% bracket, all my income gets taxed at 22%." That's not how it works. Each bracket's rate only applies to the income within that slice. If the 10% bracket covers the first $11,000 and the 12% bracket covers the next chunk, someone earning $20,000 pays 10% on the first $11,000 and 12% only on the remaining $9,000 — not 12% on the whole $20,000. Moving into a higher bracket never reduces your take-home pay; it only raises the rate on the income above the threshold.

Illustrative brackets — real thresholds change yearly and depend on filing status.
